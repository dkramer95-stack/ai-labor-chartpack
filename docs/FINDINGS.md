# AI × Labor Markets: what the data does and does not show

**Bottom line.** U.S. information-sector employment peaked in November 2022 and has fallen 12.07% since, to 95.64 on a 2019 = 100 index in September 2026 — 4.36% below its 2019 average and 4.20% below the same month a year earlier. The decline is real and large. It is not enough to conclude that AI caused it.

## The divergence

The labor market is not collapsing. Unemployment is 4.20% and payrolls are up 0.31% over 12 months. Health care & social assistance stands at 115.90 — 15.90% above its 2019 average and 25.31% above its January 2016 reading of 92.49. Professional & business services, the other large cognitive-work sector, peaked in May 2023 at 106.89 and was 105.17 in September 2026: only 1.61% below its peak and 5.17% above 2019. Manufacturing is flat (99.01 — 0.99% below 2019, 0.32% above a year earlier).

One sector broke away from that pattern. Information employment fell 12.07% from its peak while comparable sectors held or grew. That is the anomaly a displacement story would point to.

## The timing is suggestive, and that is all

The information-sector peak is November 2022 — the month ChatGPT was released. If you want to build a narrative, that is the starting point. It is not evidence. The same months contain the end of pandemic-era tech over-hiring, the sharpest rise in the cost of capital in a generation, a digital-advertising downturn and a wave of layoffs across the same firms. Generative-AI diffusion and the rate cycle are entangled in exactly the sector the chart highlights. Monthly sector data cannot separate them.

## The hiring and wage side

Openings per unemployed worker were 1.01 in August 2026 — down from a peak of 2.04 in March 2022 — with 7,079 thousand openings against 7,031 thousand unemployed. Labor demand has normalized.

Nominal wage growth was 3.02% against CPI inflation of 3.74%, so real wages fell 0.69%. That is a negative reading, but it is measured across the whole private economy: price growth outpaced pay. A real-wage decline that broad cannot be attributed to a technology concentrated in one sector.

## What would settle it

The honest position is "not yet observable." Separating AI displacement from the rate cycle and post-pandemic normalization would require an occupation-level exposure measure joined to outcomes, netting out demand shocks and firm size; evidence on hiring composition, since the predicted losses are at the entry level; and wage compression within the most exposed occupations. This pack deliberately contains none of those. It measures what moved, not why.

## Why it matters anyway

Descriptive evidence is not weak evidence; it is the evidence that constrains the story. Any claim that AI is shrinking cognitive employment has to start from the fact that information-sector employment is down 12.07% from peak while health care is up 15.90% against 2019 — and then explain why the same data also fits higher interest rates. No chart pack settles that argument. This one keeps it from being settled by assertion.
